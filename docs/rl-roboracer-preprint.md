# Learning to Race: Reward Design and Demonstration-Guided Reinforcement Learning for Autonomous Navigation

**Abstract**

We present a study of reward design and demonstration-guided reinforcement learning (RL) for an autonomous racing task in a simulated donut-course environment. Starting from a speed-reward baseline that achieves 4.16 goals/episode, we systematically ablate reward shaping, demonstration-replay architecture, and policy regularisation. Our best configuration — Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) with Advantage-Weighted Actor-Critic (AWAC) regularisation, demo-protected replay, and a goal-count reward scaled by quickness — achieves **209.22 ± 125.51 goals/episode** at **2.83 m/s**, a **50× improvement** over the baseline. We find that (1) eliminating Behavioural Cloning (BC) pre-training in favour of a demo-seeded replay buffer substantially improves final performance, (2) reward calibration between progress-shaping and goal-count objectives has a first-order effect on convergence to fast driving, and (3) AWAC regularisation consistently increases both goal throughput and driving speed relative to unconstrained SAC.

---

## 1. Introduction

Autonomous racing is a challenging benchmark for RL: the agent must simultaneously learn long-horizon navigation (following a closed-loop track), collision avoidance, and speed optimisation under real-time physics. We study this setting using a custom Unity simulation — *RL-RoboRacer* — in which a wheeled agent navigates a procedurally generated rectangular donut track. The track consists of discrete goal gates; success is measured by how many gates the agent traverses per episode before crashing.

Our contributions are:
1. An empirical ablation of five reward designs, from simple speed bonuses to calibrated goal-count rewards.
2. A demonstration of the DDPGfD-style approach — seeding the replay buffer with expert demos without BC pre-training — as a practically effective alternative to imitation pre-training.
3. Evidence that AWAC regularisation provides consistent gains in both goal throughput and driving speed in this domain.

---

## 2. Environment

**Track.** A rectangular loop generated by Unity's `TrackGenerator`. Discrete `Goal-N` trigger gates are placed at fixed intervals along the loop. An episode ends when the agent collides with a wall (`reward_failure`) or is truncated by the evaluation harness. During training, episodes are also bounded by Unity's physics step limit.

**Observation.** A 32-dimensional vector comprising: angle to the next goal, current speed, velocity heading angle, and 29 raycast distances to surrounding walls.

**Action.** A 2-dimensional continuous action: `[acceleration, steering_angle]` with acceleration ∈ `[0.1, 2.0]` and steering angle ∈ `[−1, 1]`.

**Episode termination.** Collision with any obstacle. A goal-count truncation cap (100 goals) was intended for evaluation but was not functionally applied due to a counter-semantics bug (see §6.3); all reported evaluation episodes terminate by natural crash.

**Metrics.** All reported metrics are reward-design invariant:
- **Goals / episode** (`G/ep`): total goals reached divided by episodes completed (crashes) within a trial.
- **Speed** (m/s): mean instantaneous speed, averaged over all steps within a trial.
- **Steps / goal**: episode length divided by goals per episode; lower indicates more efficient navigation.

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 Base Algorithm

All experiments use **Soft Actor-Critic (SAC)** [Haarnoja et al., 2018] with a single critic network, implemented via TF-Agents. Default hyperparameters: actor/critic/alpha learning rate 3 × 10⁻⁵, target update τ = 0.005, discount γ = 0.99, batch size 256.

### 3.2 Demonstration-Guided Replay

Expert demonstrations (≈ 50,000 steps) are collected from a hand-coded scripted policy and stored in a protected *demo table*. At training time, a mixed dataset samples 25% of batches from the demo table and 75% from the online replay buffer. A `demo_min_keep = 50,000` floor prevents the demo samples from being overwritten as the online buffer fills (capacity 300,000).

Three demo-integration strategies are compared:

| Strategy | BC pre-train steps | Demo seeding |
|---|---|---|
| Single-table control | 0 | 50k demo prefill, no min-keep floor, 0% sample ratio (demos can be overwritten) |
| Demo-protected | 0 | 50k demo prefill, 50k min-keep floor, 25% sample ratio |
| Demo-protected + AWAC | 0 | 50k demo prefill, 50k min-keep floor, 25% sample ratio + AWAC actor loss |

### 3.3 Reward Designs

Five reward functions are evaluated in sequence, each building on lessons from the previous:

**R1 — Speed-focused (baseline).** Default course reward plus a per-step speed bonus of `0.15 × speed`. Encourages forward motion but provides no explicit goal-reaching incentive.

**R2 — Progress-to-goal v1 (progress shaping).** Per-step reward proportional to reduction in distance to the next goal, plus a quickness bonus on goal arrival, a time cost (`TIME_COST = 0.005/step`), and a crash penalty (`CRASH_PENALTY = −3.0`). Designed to guide the agent toward each gate while penalising idling. Post-hoc analysis revealed that the high crash penalty caused risk-averse behaviour: the agent learned to crawl to avoid walls rather than drive fast.

**R3 — Progress-to-goal v2 (calibrated).** Same structure as R2 with recalibrated parameters: `TIME_COST = 0.03/step` (6× higher) and `CRASH_PENALTY = −1.0` (3× less harsh). The higher time cost shifts the risk/reward balance toward speed.

**R4 — Goal-count v3 (no progress term).** Removes the continuous progress term entirely. Reward is purely event-driven: a quickness-scaled goal bonus `1.0 × (0.5 + 0.5 × quickness)` where `quickness = max(0, 1 − steps_since_last_goal / 100)`, a per-step time cost (`TIME_COST = 0.02/step`), and a crash penalty (`CRASH_PENALTY = −1.0`). Eliminates "telescoping progress" artefacts that biased the value estimate.

**R5 — Goal-count v4 (calibrated time cost).** Identical structure to R4 with `TIME_COST = 0.0073/step` (reduced from R4's 0.02), calibrated so that a policy surviving a full episode at minimal speed returns approximately zero — eliminating the degenerate survival strategy. Scaled by `reward_scale_factor = 10` in the SAC objective to improve signal-to-noise in the entropy term. The `env_discount = 1.0` (no per-step environment discount on top of γ) is used for all No-BC and AWAC arms to give the agent a full-horizon view of its actions.

### 3.4 AWAC Regularisation

Advantage-Weighted Actor Critic (AWAC) [Nair et al., 2020] regularises the actor update toward demo actions, weighted by their advantage over the current policy. We add the following supplementary loss to the SAC actor objective:

```
L_AWAC = λ · E_{(s,a)~demos}[w(s,a) · ||π(s) − a||²]
```

where `w(s,a) = min(exp(A(s,a) / β), 20)` (stop-gradient), β = 1.0, and `A(s,a) = Q(s,a_demo) − Q(s,a_π)` using the single critic Q-value. We use an **MSE regression** toward the demo action rather than the log-likelihood used in the original AWAC paper; this is numerically stable when demo actions lie at the action-space bounds (a known failure mode for tanh-squashed log-likelihood). Two variants are evaluated: constant λ = 0.5 (`AWAC`), and an exponentially decaying λ from 0.5 → 0 over **120,000 training steps** (`AWAC-decay`), after which the actor trains as standard SAC.

## 4. Experimental Setup

All models are trained for 100,000 iterations (baselines) or 250,000 iterations (No-BC and AWAC configurations), using 2 parallel Unity gym actors. Evaluation uses a separate standardised protocol: 5 trials of 10 episodes each (n = 10–15 pooled trial-means per arm after two evaluation rounds). A 100-goal-per-episode truncation cap was implemented in the evaluation harness but was not functionally applied: the counter used to track in-episode goals (`goals_per_episode_total`) is only updated at episode termination (crash), not continuously during an episode, so the cap's trigger condition was never satisfied. All 10 episodes per trial terminated by natural crash. The reported metrics therefore reflect genuine crash-limited episode performance. All reported statistics are means over pooled trial-means; standard deviations reflect trial-to-trial variance.

### 5.2 Speed and Efficiency

The AWAC + R5 arm achieves the highest speed (2.83 m/s) and lowest steps-per-goal (26.9), approximately 1.9× the speed of the demo-protected baselines (≈ 1.0–1.1 m/s) and half as many steps per goal. The AWAC-decay arm also shows substantially elevated speed (2.61 m/s) relative to its non-AWAC counterpart with the same reward (No-BC DDPGfD + R5: 1.44 m/s), suggesting that AWAC regularisation promotes active driving behaviour rather than conservative crawling.

### 5.3 Ablation: Demo-Protection

Comparing the speed baseline (R1, n=15, G/ep = 4.16) against the single-table control (R1, n=10, G/ep = 10.56) isolates the effect of demo prefilling and replay capacity. Both arms use R1 (speed reward); the single-table arm additionally prefills 50,000 demo steps into a 300,000-step replay buffer (with no min-keep floor, so demos are eventually overwritten). The 2.5× gain reflects the benefit of warm-starting the replay buffer with expert experience. The demo-protected arm with reward R3 (n=10, G/ep = 53.93) adds a further ~5× improvement, attributable to both the protected demo table (25% permanent sampling) and the improved reward calibration — these two factors cannot be decoupled in this ablation.

### 5.4 Ablation: BC Pre-Training vs. DDPGfD

The standard demo-protected arms use the same replay design as the No-BC DDPGfD arms, differing only in BC pre-training (default 50,000 steps of imitation) and training budget (100k vs. 250k iterations). Controlling for reward design is not possible across this ablation (the BC arms used R2/R3, the No-BC arms used R4/R5). The No-BC DDPGfD arm with R4 (G/ep = 30.95) underperforms the demo-protected R3 arm (G/ep = 53.93) despite 2.5× more training; however, the No-BC DDPGfD + R5 arm (G/ep = 49.74) is closer, and both No-BC arms exhibit much higher speed (1.4–1.5 m/s) than the BC demo-protected arms (≈ 1.0 m/s), suggesting BC pre-training may instil conservative driving habits that persist into RL fine-tuning.

### 5.5 Ablation: AWAC Regularisation

Comparing No-BC DDPGfD + R5 (G/ep = 49.74 ± 42.02, speed = 1.44 m/s) against AWAC + R5 (G/ep = 209.22 ± 125.51, speed = 2.83 m/s) isolates the contribution of AWAC actor regularisation. The 4.2× gain in goal throughput and 2× speed increase suggest AWAC substantially improves policy quality in this domain.

The AWAC-decay arm (70.28 ± 33.95, 2.61 m/s) substantially underperforms constant AWAC despite sharing the same architecture, reward, and demo buffer, suggesting the decay schedule — λ annealing from 0.5 to 0 over the first 120,000 of 250,000 training steps — is premature. We hypothesise three interacting mechanisms:

1. **Dense vs. sparse gradients.** The v4 reward is sparse (goal-crossing events only); between goals the per-step signal is −0.0073. The AWAC term, by contrast, provides a dense MSE gradient toward expert actions at every demo-sampled step. When λ decays to zero at the training midpoint, the dense signal disappears and the SAC critic — still learning from sparse events — has insufficient gradient to maintain the fast-driving behaviour unaided.

2. **Q-function distribution shift.** The SAC critic is trained throughout to estimate Q-values for an AWAC-regularised policy. When regularisation is removed at step 120,000 the policy distribution shifts, but the critic does not immediately recalibrate. The resulting Q-value mismatch degrades actor updates during the remaining 130,000 steps.

3. **SAC entropy vs. deterministic driving.** SAC's entropy bonus encourages action diversity, which is counterproductive in a task where the optimal policy is near-deterministic (high acceleration, precise steering). The AWAC term continuously counteracts this by pulling the actor toward expert (low-entropy) actions. Once λ → 0, the entropy bonus goes unchecked and the policy drifts toward higher-variance, lower-goal-throughput behaviour.

Together these suggest that in domains with sparse rewards and near-optimal demonstrations, **constant demo regularisation dominates any fixed decay schedule** because the premise underlying decay — that the online policy will eventually surpass the demos — may not be reached within the training budget. A natural follow-up experiment is to anneal λ over the full 250,000 steps, or to condition the decay rate on the measured advantage of demo actions (stop decaying when the online policy consistently exceeds the demos on the critic's estimate).

## 6. Discussion

### 6.1 Role of Reward Design

The progression from R1 (speed bonus) through R5 (calibrated goal-count) illustrates that reward design is a first-order concern for this task. Two failure modes were identified and corrected:

1. **Risk aversion** (R1 → R2 transition): The high crash penalty relative to the time cost made cautious driving optimal. R2 raised `TIME_COST` by **6×** (0.005 → 0.03) and softened `CRASH_PENALTY` by **3×** (−3.0 → −1.0), shifting the risk/reward balance decisively toward speed.

2. **Telescoping progress** (R2 → R4 transition): The continuous per-step progress reward depended on cumulative position along the track, creating a non-stationary value target that slowed learning. Replacing it with discrete goal-event rewards stabilised training.

The final calibration step (R4 → R5) ensured that zero-speed survival was net-negative, eliminating a degenerate but locally optimal policy.

### 6.2 Variance and Stability

Several arms show high trial-to-trial variance (e.g., No-BC DDPGfD + R5: SD/mean ≈ 0.85; AWAC + R5: SD/mean ≈ 0.60). The variance reflects genuine policy non-stationarity: each trial begins from a random track position, and the agent's performance varies substantially with starting position. Future work should use a fixed set of starting positions for evaluation.

### 6.3 Limitations

- **Single-seed evaluation.** All training runs use a single random seed. The reported variance is over evaluation trials (random start positions), not over training seeds.
- **No comparison against domain-randomised or curriculum baselines.**
- **Goal-truncation cap not applied.** A 100-goal episode truncation was implemented to bound evaluation time for strong policies, but was not functionally applied in any eval run: the counter used to track mid-episode goal progress (`goals_per_episode_total`) is only incremented at episode termination (crash), not as goals are reached. The truncation trigger therefore never fired. All episodes terminated by natural crash; reported goal-per-episode statistics reflect true crash-limited performance and are not artificially capped. A correct implementation would track goals reached during the current episode via a continuously updated in-episode counter (e.g., `goals_reached` in `DonutCourse`) rather than the cumulative post-crash accumulator.
- **Single critic.** We use a single critic network rather than the twin-critic variant from the original SAC paper [Haarnoja et al., 2018]. Twin critics reduce Q-value overestimation by taking the minimum of two independent estimates; our single-critic setup may overestimate Q-values, which can inflate the AWAC advantage weights and destabilise the regularisation term. The strong empirical results suggest this did not prevent learning, but the single-critic configuration is a deviation from standard practice that future work should ablate.
- **Track geometry fixed.** All results are for a single track configuration (`corner_radius = 10`, `curvature_difficulty = 0`). Generalisation to harder geometries is untested.

---

## 7. Conclusion

We demonstrate a 50× improvement in autonomous racing performance over a speed-reward baseline through the combination of (a) calibrated goal-count reward design, (b) demonstration-seeded replay without BC pre-training, and (c) AWAC actor regularisation. The key finding is that reward calibration and policy regularisation interact strongly: unconstrained SAC with the same reward and demo buffer achieves only 49.74 goals/episode, while AWAC regularisation pushes this to 209.22. The results motivate further investigation of advantage-weighted demo regularisation in continuous control tasks where cold-start exploration is the primary bottleneck.
